# Designing rental applications for an AI-native leasing platform

RentEngine is a seed-stage startup that helps rental agents and property managers lease their properties and manage their leads in one place. I've been working with them as the founding designer since August 2025, taking equal parts design strategy and execution — usually starting with the CEO to unpack the purpose and value of a product, and taking it all the way through to functional prototypes for engineering.

This case study covers Rental Applications, a leasing and application management product that lets property managers review, approve, reject, and screen applicants for units currently on the market. It became a flagship feature for the company, sold as an upsell over the core subscription, and drove a significant uptake in customer demos and new business.

## Who the product is for

RentEngine's users are property managers. Most are smaller operators, though the company is actively expanding into the large multifamily space. Rental Applications was partly designed to bridge that gap — to give smaller managers a product that scales with them, and to give larger ones a workflow they can adopt without changing the rest of their stack.

The user base shaped the design more than any other input. Property managers in this segment tend to be older and less technologically fluent than typical SaaS users. The product needed clear signalling, high accessibility, and interactions that didn't require learning. Experimental UI was off the table.

## The core tradeoff: how much to show, when

The hardest design problem on this project wasn't a feature — it was a question of information disclosure.

Some property managers want to see everything about an applicant upfront. Credit, employment, references, screening results, prior tenancy, the lot. They review applications in batches and scan for red flags. Other managers prefer to start with a summary and drill down only when something warrants attention. The same surface had to serve both.

Progressive disclosure was the obvious answer, but the version that works for one user often frustrates the other. Show too little upfront and the batch-scanners feel like the product is hiding things. Show too much and the drill-down users feel overwhelmed. Getting this right took several rounds of prototyping, real conversations with property managers, and a few rejected directions before we landed somewhere that worked for both groups.

The version that shipped uses a layered approach. The top of an applicant view shows the facts most managers said they needed to make a first-pass judgement — income-to-rent ratio, screening status, application completeness, key flags. Everything else is one click away rather than hidden. Scanners get what they need without scrolling; drill-down users get full depth without friction.

## Designing for an AI-native product

I touched the AI agent surfaces briefly on this project, mostly around the visual signalling of AI. The work was less about the agent itself and more about how AI is communicated to the user — feedback, affordances, and distinguishing AI-generated content from computed or user-entered content.

The thing that's different about designing for an AI-native product is transparency. Users need to know what the product is doing behind the scenes, what's been generated by AI versus what's been computed deterministically, and that AI-generated information can be wrong. None of these are concerns in a traditional SaaS product. In an AI-native one, they're central — get them wrong and users lose trust in the entire product, not just the AI features.

For Rental Applications, this meant being deliberate about where AI surfaces appeared, what they were allowed to claim, and how the user could correct or override them. The visual language for AI-touched content stayed consistent across the product, so users always knew when they were looking at something the AI had contributed to.

## Working async with a US team

Working as a senior IC from South Africa on a US product is something I've found to work well. The time difference can be used deliberately — projects kick off late in the US day, get iterated on overnight my time, and are back with the team before they start their morning. The cadence ends up favouring small, frequent deliveries over long check-ins, which suits the design work itself.

## What shipped, and what happened

Rental Applications shipped to customers and became one of RentEngine's flagship features, sold as an upsell over the core subscription. The company saw a meaningful uptake in customer demos and new business after the feature went live — the upsell motion gave the sales team a fresh anchor for conversations, and the product itself was substantive enough to close them.

## Reflection

The thing this project clarified is that information disclosure in B2B products is more often a user-segmentation problem than a UI problem. The instinct is to design a single hierarchy and trust progressive disclosure to handle the rest. But when two segments of the same user base want fundamentally different things from the same surface, the work is in finding the structure that serves both — not in hiding the difference.

The AI work, brief as it was, reinforced something I'd started to suspect at NYSHEX: the visual language for AI is becoming as central as the visual language for state was a decade ago. Users need to understand what's AI, what's computed, what's editable, what's authoritative — and they need to understand it without thinking about it. We're early in the patterns for this. The products that get it right will earn user trust in a way that's hard to replicate after the fact.
